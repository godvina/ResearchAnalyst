# Documentary Research Format — Best Practices

## Based on: History Channel, Discovery Channel, National Geographic production methodology

---

## The Documentary Research Stack

A compelling documentary investigation follows a 5-layer evidence structure. Each layer builds credibility with the audience and production stakeholders.

### Layer 1: THE HOOK (30 seconds)
What makes this worth investigating? One provocative fact that challenges assumptions.

**Template:**
> "[Specific measurement/statistic] that shouldn't exist unless [implication]."

**Example:**
> "The St Michael's Line runs 364 miles from St Michael's Mount to Hopton-on-Sea. Seven major sacred sites built across 3,000 years of history sit within 200 meters of this single straight line — a precision that predates modern surveying by millennia."

### Layer 2: THE ESTABLISHED FACTS (2 minutes)
What do credentialed researchers agree on? Published, peer-reviewed, or institutionally-backed data.

**Required sources (in credibility order):**
1. Academic publications (JSTOR, Google Scholar, university press)
2. Government surveys (USGS, Ordnance Survey, national archaeological registries)
3. Museum archives and expedition records
4. Satellite/LiDAR data (NASA, ESA, university datasets)
5. Published books by credentialed researchers (with citations)

**Template:**
```
ESTABLISHED FACTS:
• [Fact 1] — Source: [Institution, Year]
• [Fact 2] — Source: [Institution, Year]
• What mainstream archaeology accepts: [summary]
• What remains unexplained: [the gap]
```

### Layer 3: THE ANOMALY (3 minutes)
What doesn't fit the conventional explanation? This is where the investigation begins.

**Criteria for a valid anomaly:**
- Measurable (not anecdotal)
- Reproducible (others can verify)
- Unexplained by current models (or inadequately explained)
- Not debunked (address known counter-arguments)

**Template:**
```
THE ANOMALY:
• Observation: [what was measured/found]
• Expected (by mainstream model): [what should be there]
• Actual: [what IS there]
• Discrepancy: [quantified gap]
• Counter-arguments considered: [list + why they're insufficient]
```

### Layer 4: THE PATTERN (5 minutes)
One anomaly is a curiosity. Multiple anomalies forming a pattern is a story.

**Documentary gold = patterns across:**
- Geography (same thing at distant locations)
- Time (same thing across different eras)
- Culture (same thing across unrelated civilizations)
- Scale (same ratio/geometry at micro and macro levels)

**Template:**
```
THE PATTERN:
• Sites exhibiting this: [list with coordinates]
• Geographic span: [km]
• Time span: [years]
• Cultural independence: [evidence they didn't communicate]
• Statistical significance: [probability of random occurrence]
• Visualization: [map/chart description]
```

### Layer 5: THE IMPLICATION (1 minute)
If this pattern is real, what does it mean? This is NOT a conclusion — it's a question for further investigation.

**Template:**
> "If [pattern] is not coincidence, then [implication A] or [implication B]. The next step is [specific investigation that would confirm or deny]."

## Documentary Credibility Framework

### The "Three Source Rule"
Every major claim needs three independent sources:
1. A measurement or dataset
2. A published researcher who noted it
3. A counter-example or control (what does a random sample look like?)

### The "Skeptic Paragraph"
Every section MUST include the strongest counter-argument:
- "Critics argue that..."
- "The conventional explanation is..."
- "This could be explained by [mundane cause] if..."

Then explain why it's insufficient (or acknowledge when it IS sufficient).

### The "Expert Talking Head" Rule
For every claim, identify:
- Who would support this on camera? (Name, institution, publication)
- Who would argue against it? (Name, institution, publication)
- What would change their mind?

## Visual Storytelling Standards

### Maps
- Always show scale bar
- Always show what "random" looks like for comparison
- Use animation: reveal patterns sequentially (don't show everything at once)
- Color-code by confidence (green = measured, blue = probable, grey = theoretical)

### Data Visualization
- Start with the simplest possible view
- Add complexity ONE layer at a time
- Let the viewer's eye track from "huh, that's interesting" to "wait, ALL of these?"
- Never show a pattern without showing what random noise looks like for comparison

### The "So What?" Test
After every segment, the producer asks: "Why should I care?"
- If the answer is "it's interesting" — not enough
- If the answer is "it changes how we understand [X]" — we have a story

## Research Execution Checklist

For each site/node in an investigation:

- [ ] **Coordinates confirmed** (multiple sources agree on location)
- [ ] **Dating established** (when was it built/used — radiocarbon, stratigraphy, historical record)
- [ ] **Construction method documented** (how was it built — materials, techniques, tools)
- [ ] **Cultural context mapped** (who built it — civilization, era, known capabilities)
- [ ] **Anomaly identified** (what doesn't fit the expected model)
- [ ] **Measurement cited** (precision, alignment, dimensions — with source)
- [ ] **Counter-argument addressed** (why the mundane explanation is insufficient)
- [ ] **Connection to other sites documented** (shared techniques, alignments, proportions)
- [ ] **Expert sources identified** (who published on this — name, institution, year)
- [ ] **Visual assets located** (satellite imagery, photographs, diagrams — with rights)

## Applying This to Ley Line Research

### What we need for each documented ley line:

1. **Origin research**: Who first documented this alignment? When? What was their methodology?
2. **Precision measurement**: How close are the sites to the mathematical line? (arcminutes)
3. **Age diversity**: Do the sites span multiple eras? (proves it's not one culture's road)
4. **Function diversity**: Are they different types? (church, stone circle, holy well, hill fort)
5. **Statistical control**: How many random lines through England hit this many sites?
6. **Modern surveys**: Has anyone measured geomagnetic anomalies along the line?
7. **Continuity evidence**: Did later builders KNOW about the line? (church built on pagan site)

### What we need for the global grid theory:

1. **All 62 vertices characterized** (what's at each — site, ocean, unexplored)
2. **Distance metrics** (how far is each known site from its nearest vertex — in km)
3. **Random baseline** (if you drop 62 random points, how many land near ancient sites?)
4. **Cross-cultural independence** (prove the sites aren't connected by trade/migration)
5. **Geometric precision** (do the vertices actually form perfect icosahedral angles?)

## Template: Documentary Episode Brief

```
EPISODE: [Title — max 8 words]
LOGLINE: [One sentence that makes someone click play]

COLD OPEN (30s):
[The most visually stunning anomaly — aerial shot, dramatic reveal]

ACT 1 — THE MYSTERY (8 min):
- What is known (established archaeology)
- What shouldn't exist (the anomaly)
- Why conventional explanation fails

ACT 2 — THE PATTERN (12 min):
- Site visits (3-4 locations)
- Measurements and data
- Expert interviews (2 per side)
- The "aha" moment where the pattern clicks

ACT 3 — THE IMPLICATION (8 min):
- If this is real, what does it mean?
- What we'd need to prove it
- The next investigation this leads to
- "This changes everything" vs "here's what we still don't know"

PRODUCTION NEEDS:
- Drone/aerial: [locations]
- Expert interviews: [names]
- Archive footage: [what]
- Graphics/VFX: [map animations, reconstructions]
- Field measurements: [what equipment]
```
